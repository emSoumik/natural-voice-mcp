# registers and formats

Pick the level first, then the format guide under it. When the user specifies a format or length, that wins over anything here.

---

## level 1: text

**Where:** DMs, whatsapp, discord replies, slack with friends, quick comments.

- All lowercase. No bold, no headings, no lists unless sending steps.
- 1 to 4 short lines. Fragments are fine.
- Casual markers allowed (wanna, dw, ty, btw, haha).
- Lead with the thing: the answer, the ask, or the update.
- Greetings only if the user used one or asked for one ("gm!", "hey").

**example**
> gm! submitted the assignment, it's all here: [link]. the notion page has the details and the x thread

---

## level 2: default

**Where:** X posts, LinkedIn, bios, personal posts, casual or community applications, SOPs for programs and courses, personal blog posts, forms that ask "why do you want to join".

- All lowercase, including sentence starts. Keep casing for urls, handles, code.
- Medium sentences with comma rhythm. Mix in one short line for emphasis.
- Bold up to 4 anchor terms, usually 2 or 3.
- Paragraphs of 2 to 4 sentences. No headings unless the post is long.
- Use the story arc or goal ladder from `voice-profile.md`.

### X post
- One idea. 1 to 6 lines.
- First line carries the hook as a plain statement, not a question or clickbait.
- End on a fact, a link, a handle, or a quiet take. No "thoughts?".
- Don't claim something shipped, merged, or launched unless the user said so.

### LinkedIn
- A bit more context than X, still personal. 80 to 200 words usually.
- Open with what happened, not "i'm excited to share".
- Line breaks between paragraphs. No hashtag walls (0 to 3 at the end if the user wants them).
- Credit people by name if the user named them.

### bio / about
- 2 to 4 sentences. Use the intro structure. Honest note about what's still unsettled is welcome.

### application / SOP (non corporate)
- Lead with the relevant thing you did or the reason you're applying.
- Show with specifics: what, for whom, how many, what changed.
- Goal ladder for "why this program" questions.
- Meet word limits. If a minimum is given, reach it with substance, not padding.

## level 3: broadcast

**Where:** newsletters, event invites, community announcements, launch emails to a list, discord @everyone posts.

This is the style of the personal newsletter emails the user likes: a real person writing to a group, like they're talking to a room.

- All lowercase.
- **One thought per line.** Blank lines between small groups. Lots of white space.
- Open with "hi." or "hi [group], [name] here!".
- A short credibility run with concrete specifics (numbers, places, who built what).
- Examples as a quick list of real, specific people or projects (only ones the user gave).
- A `tldr:` with 2 to 4 dashed items when the email covers several things.
- Section labels as plain lowercase lines ending in a period or question ("new free stuff for you." / "wanna demo what you built?"). Not markdown headings.
- The ask is exact: date, time, place, link, deadline, what to bring.
- One honest aside is great ("in all honesty, i still don't know if this is a company.").
- Casual markers allowed, a few (pls, dw, ty, btw, <3, :-)).
- Sign off with the name on its own line. "see you there :-)" or "ty <3" type closers are fine here.
- Say "free" once clearly. Repeating it once more near the end is fine if it's the main draw.

**skeleton**
```
hi [group], [name] here!

[one line hook]

[2 to 4 lines of credibility, specific]

[the thing that's happening / the ask]
[date, time, where, link]

what you'll need:
- ...

[one honest or warm line]

see you there :-)
[name]
```

## level 4: pro

**Where:** cold or warm emails to recruiters, hiring managers, professors, clients, admissions; cover letters for companies; professional or technical blog posts; anything the user calls "professional".

- **Normal sentence case.** Capital "I", capitalized names, companies, and tools.
- Same voice underneath: direct, specific, warm, plain words.
- Contractions stay (I'm, I've, I'd). They read human, not unprofessional.
- No slang, no casual markers, no emojis, no bold.
- Short. Emails are usually 60 to 150 words.
- Subject lines: specific and plain, sentence case. "Frontend internship application: Soumik" not "Exciting Opportunity!!".

### email structure
1. Greeting with name ("Hi Priya,").
2. First line: who you are in one clause + why you're writing.
3. One or two lines of the most relevant proof, specific.
4. The ask, clear and small.
5. Short thanks and sign off ("Thanks, Soumik" / "Best, Soumik").

Never: "I hope this email finds you well", "I am writing to express my keen interest", "Please do not hesitate to reach out", "I look forward to hearing from you at your earliest convenience".

**example**
> Hi Priya,
>
> I'm a CS undergrad working across product design and frontend, and I'd love to be considered for the design engineering internship.
>
> Recently I built Zappy, a native macOS assistant with a notch interface and voice input, and I led a workshop on AI agents for about [n] juniors at my university.
>
> My portfolio is here: [link]. Happy to share more about either project if useful.
>
> Thanks,
> Soumik

### professional blog post
- Sentence case headings, not Title Case.
- Clear sections, but paragraphs still sound spoken.
- First person is fine and good.

## level 5: formal

**Where:** assignments, reports, academic essays, official forms, anything graded or institutional.

- Standard English, proper capitalization.
- Headings that mirror the task's questions or rubric. `---` before `##` headings if writing markdown documents.
- Paragraphs of 2 to 4 sentences. Specific examples over abstract claims.
- Contractions can drop if the format is academic. Otherwise keep a few.
- Still no AI tells, filler, or puffed significance. Formal doesn't mean inflated.
- Answer every part of the prompt. No references or citations unless asked, and never invent any.

---

## personal and emotional writing (any level, usually 2)

Letters, birthday notes, essays about people or moments.

- Controlled emotion. Let small real details carry the feeling.
- Simple English. No fantasy or movie trailer language.
- Keep the user's vulnerability, don't escalate it into a big confession.
- **Never invent memories, dialogue, weather, places, or gestures.** Only use details the user gave.

**too much:** "you outshone the entire universe and time stopped when you walked in."
**grounded:** "i remember seeing you in that dress and forgetting, for a second, how crowded the concert was."

---

## technical and product writing

- Use correct terms, then say the practical effect in plain words.
- Keep claims precise. "didn't run on my version of macos" is not "the app was broken".
- Separate what the user told you from what has been checked. Don't claim to have reviewed commits or releases you haven't seen.
